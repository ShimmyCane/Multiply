About Multiplication 2.0

Multiplication isn't one idea. It's several pictures that all give the same answer. Each view in 2.0 shows one of those pictures. You can play with it using as few words as possible, and the numbers stay the same when you switch views, so you can see that 3 × 4 is 12 every way you look at it.

Where the ideas came from

The Math Learning Center: Number Pieces

Array model article

What they do well: Free virtual base-ten pieces that you build into real arrays. An edge piece works like a measuring tape, so the two sides of the array are always visible.

What we took: The Array and Split views. Each side of the rectangle is labelled with its length, and you can cut the rectangle into parts and watch the partial products add back up.

Mathigon / Amplify Polypad

What they do well: More than 50 manipulatives (number lines, number bars, dot arrangements, prime factor circles), all on one playful canvas with very little text.

What we took: The "almost no words" goal, number-line hops for Jumps, and the idea that dot pictures can show factors.

Whizz: How the Japanese multiplication method works

Make Math Moments The Kid Should See This

What they do well: They don't treat it as a magic trick. Each cluster of crossings is one partial product, and Make Math Moments links the lines back to arrays and base-ten blocks.

What we took: Sticks colors each cluster by place value (hundreds, tens, ones) and shows the counts under the clusters. With one digit, the crossings are just an array turned on its corner.

Red Blob Games: Mathologer modulo circle

Mathologer video Wolfram Demonstration

What they do well: Burkard Polster's "Times Tables, Mandelbrot and the Heart of Mathematics" turns the 2 times table into a cardioid. Red Blob's version makes it a toy you control with just two sliders.

What we took: Circle keeps the two sliders (points and multiplier), adds a play button that sweeps the multiplier, and highlights one chord, 3 → 3 × m, so a child can see that each line really is a times-table fact.

Brent Yorgey: Factorization diagrams

Interactive version (David Peter) Math Munch: Factor Conga

What they do well: Each number is drawn as nested rings of dots. Twelve is three groups of four, which is three groups of two groups of two. You can see a number's prime factors before anyone names them.

What we took: Dots starts with "a groups of b" (a ring of rings) and has a "primes" button that breaks it all the way down. The dots are colored by outer group, so you can still count the groups.

University of Kansas: Classic geometry problems (PDF)

A Geometric Approach to Defining Multiplication

What they do well: They show the classic straightedge construction. With a unit length 1, two lengths a and b, and one pair of parallel lines, you get a length of exactly a×b. No areas or counting needed.

What we took: Triangles lets you drag a and b, even to fractions. The play button slides the parallel line from 1 out to b, so you watch the small triangle grow into the big one.

Oak National Academy: Multiplication as scaling

What they do well: They describe multiplying as stretching by a scale factor. That's the picture that still works for ½ or 1½, when "groups of" stops making sense.

What we took: Stretch has two rulers joined by fan lines. Drag the bottom ruler and watch every number stretch at once. The orange line shows where 1 goes, because the multiplier is what 1 turns into.

How we put it together

Each view answers the same question: what does a × b look like? We kept a few rules:

🎨 Same colors everywhere. The first number is red and the second is blue, in every view.

👆 Touch first. You tap, drag or press ▶. You never have to read instructions.

🔁 Same answer, different picture. Your numbers carry over when you switch views, so the same fact appears ten different ways.

🧱 Easy to grow. Each view is one small, separate block of code in multiply2.html, so a new picture can be added without touching the others.
